# California City Public Records Request — Process Flow (Email-Based)

**Scope:** Requests for *copies* of records handled by email with a California city, under the California Public Records Act (CPRA) (Gov. Code § 7920.000 et seq. (recodified by AB 473, Stats. 2021, ch. 614, operative 1/1/2023; formerly § 6250 et seq.)). Inspection-of-records rules excluded per scope.

**Governing clock:** CPRA (Gov. Code 7920.000 et seq.): 10 calendar days to issue determination, +14 by written notice in unusual circumstances (incl. cyberattack, AB 370). Production must be 'prompt' — no fixed day count. Fees capped at direct duplication cost; no admin appeal; mandatory fees if requester wins.

---

## PHASE 1 — RECEIPT & INTAKE

**1.1 Request received by email.**
Any person may request records — no California residency requirement ("any person," § 7922.530(a); "person" broadly defined at § 7920.520; out-of-state and non-U.S. requesters routinely use the CPRA). Requesters may be anonymous or pseudonymous; agencies may ask for contact info to deliver records but cannot require proof of identity. Purpose may not be demanded or used to limit disclosure: § 7921.300 (former § 6257.5) — "This division does not allow limitations on access to a public record based upon the purpose for which the record is being requested, if the record is otherwise subject to disclosure" (text verified). Narrow exceptions exist for specific record types (e.g., arrestee/victim address information under §§ 7923.600 et seq. is available only to specified categories upon declaration of permitted, non-commercial use).

**Commercial-purpose treatment:** Essentially none for ordinary records: no commercial-purpose disclosure requirement, no different deadline, and no higher fee tier for commercial requesters — § 7921.300 bars purpose-based limits. Narrow statutory carve-outs: arrestee/crime-victim address information (§§ 7923.600 et seq., former § 6254(f)(3)) is available only to specified categories (journalists, scholars, etc.) upon declaration that use is not commercial and the information will not be resold; similar limits govern some licensee lists. These do not affect a generic city email copy request.

**1.2 Log and acknowledge.**
No separate statutory acknowledgment duty — the 10-day determination under § 7922.535(a) is itself the required first response. City practice: most cities (e.g., NextRequest/GovQA/JustFOIA portal cities) send automatic acknowledgment emails immediately; this is practice, not statute.

**1.3 Response deadline framework.**
Within 10 days of receipt (statute says "10 days"; universally applied as CALENDAR days), the agency must make a DETERMINATION whether the request seeks disclosable public records and promptly notify the requester of the determination and the reasons therefor; if the determination is that disclosable records exist, the notice must also state the estimated date and time when the records will be made available (Gov. Code § 7922.535(a) — estimated-date sentence verified in subd. (a)). This is a determination deadline, not a production deadline — production must be "prompt" (§ 7922.530(a)) and the Act may not be construed to permit delay or obstruction (§ 7922.500), but no fixed day count applies to actual delivery. In "unusual circumstances" the deadline may be extended by written notice, setting forth the reasons and the date of determination, by no more than 14 additional days (24 max) (§ 7922.535(b)). Unusual circumstances, as amended by AB 370 (Stats. 2025, ch. 34, eff. 1/1/2026), § 7922.535(c)(1)-(6): (1) need to search for and collect records from field facilities or other establishments separate from the office processing the request; (2) voluminous amount of separate and distinct records demanded in a single request; (3) consultation with another agency having substantial interest, or among two or more components of the agency; (4) need to compile data, write programming language or a computer program, or construct a computer report to extract data; (5) NEW per AB 370: inability of the agency, because of a cyberattack, to access its electronic servers or systems to search for and obtain a record maintained on those servers/systems in electronic format — the extension applies only until the agency regains access, and does not cover records held on paper or on unaffected systems; (6) a Governor-proclaimed state of emergency in the agency's jurisdiction that "currently and directly" affects the agency's ability to timely respond due to staffing shortages or closure of facilities where the records are located (AB 370 added "and directly" to the pre-existing "currently" standard). No statutory deemed-denial provision; failure to respond is treated by courts and practice as a constructive denial permitting immediate suit under § 7923.000.

## PHASE 2 — PATH: VAGUE / OVERBROAD REQUEST

Statutory duty to assist (§ 7922.600, former § 6253.1; text verified): to the extent reasonable under the circumstances, the agency must (1) assist the requester in identifying records and information responsive to the request or its stated purpose, (2) describe the information technology and physical location in which the records exist, and (3) provide suggestions for overcoming any practical basis for denying access. Item (1) is deemed satisfied if the agency cannot identify the information after a reasonable effort to elicit clarifying information (§ 7922.600(b)). The duty does not apply if the agency makes the records available, makes an index of its records available, or denies the request based solely on a listed exemption (§ 7922.605). Requests need only "reasonably describe an identifiable record" (§ 7922.530(a)). The duty operates within the same 10-day window; no statutory tolling for clarification — cities commonly treat a clarified request as restarting the 10-day clock, but that is practice, not statute.

**No response from requester:** after a policy waiting period, closure notice ("administratively closed; may resubmit"). **[Practice]**

## PHASE 3 — SEARCH & LOCATE

Reasonable, good-faith search; route to custodian department(s).

**PATH: NO RESPONSIVE RECORDS —** If no responsive records exist, the § 7922.535 determination should say so within 10 days; the CPRA covers only records "prepared, owned, used, or retained" by the agency (§ 7920.530) and courts hold there is no duty to create a record that does not exist or to answer questions. Exception blurring the line: an agency may construct/compile a record from electronic data if the requester pays the full cost including programming and computer services (§ 7922.575(b)), but it is not obligated to. Adequacy of search can be litigated; agencies should describe search efforts when challenged (duty-to-assist § 7922.600(a)(2) also requires describing where records exist).

**PATH: Another agency's records —** courtesy referral. **[Practice]**

## PHASE 4 — REVIEW, REDACTION & THE DENIAL PATH

**4.1 Review & redaction.**
Strong segregability duty: any reasonably segregable portion of a record must be made available after deletion of the exempt portions (§ 7922.525(b), former § 6253(a)). Redaction labor is NOT chargeable: only the "direct cost of duplication" may be recovered for copies (§ 7922.530(a)), which excludes search, review, and redaction time (North County Parents Org. v. Dept. of Education (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 144); for electronic records, National Lawyers Guild v. City of Hayward (2020) 9 Cal.5th 488 held redaction (there, editing body-cam video) is not chargeable "data extraction" under former § 6253.9(b)(2), now § 7922.575(b).

**4.2 PATH: DENIAL — required communications.**
Any denial in whole or part of a written request must be in writing (§ 7922.540(a)); the notification of denial must set forth the names and titles or positions of each person responsible for the denial (§ 7922.540(b)); and the agency must justify withholding by complying with § 7922.000 (§ 7922.540(c)) — i.e., demonstrating a specific exemption (Part 5, §§ 7930.000 et seq.) or that on the facts the public interest served by nondisclosure clearly outweighs disclosure (catchall, former § 6255). Under Cal. Const. art. I, § 3(b)(2) (Prop 59, 2004), statutes limiting access are construed narrowly and access statutes broadly. No Vaughn-index requirement, though courts may examine records in camera (§ 7923.105; Evid. Code § 915). Denial deadline is the same 10(+14)-day determination window.

**Administrative appeal:** None — judicial only. California has no state FOI ombudsperson, public access counselor, or AG appeal route for CPRA denials (an ombudsperson bill, AB 343 of 2021-22, never became law; the 2025 AB 343 is unrelated, expanding official-address privacy). A few charter cities create LOCAL review bodies by ordinance (e.g., San Francisco's Sunshine Ordinance Task Force), but these are city-specific and not part of the CPRA.

## PHASE 5 — FEES, DEPOSITS & PAYMENT (summary level)

**5.1 Fee structure.**
Statute sets no cents-per-page figure — copies are provided "upon payment of fees covering direct costs of duplication, or a statutory fee if applicable" (§ 7922.530(a), text verified). "Direct cost" means the actual duplication cost (copier run cost plus the operator's time actually spent copying), roughly $0.10-$0.25/page in common practice; it excludes search, retrieval, review, redaction, and overhead (North County Parents). Requesters may also inspect free and use their own camera/scanner on site at no charge, subject to reasonable protective limits (§ 7922.530(b)-(c)). Electronic records: agency must make the information available in any electronic format in which it holds it, and provide a copy in the requested format if that format has been used by the agency (§ 7922.570); it may charge only the direct cost of producing the electronic copy (§ 7922.575(a)) — for existing electronic files delivered by email this is typically $0. Full cost INCLUDING construction, programming, and computer services is chargeable only when (1) the record is one produced only at otherwise regularly scheduled intervals, or (2) the request would require data compilation, extraction, or programming to produce the record (§ 7922.575(b), text verified). Separate statutory fees override for specific records (e.g., campaign statements/statements of economic interests at $0.10/page plus $5 retrieval fee for reports 5+ years old, Gov. Code § 81008). Flat city fee schedules exceeding true direct cost (e.g., $1.00 first page) are contested by the First Amendment Coalition and not definitively adjudicated.

**Labor charges:** Search, retrieval, review, and redaction staff time is NOT chargeable — no threshold or free-hours concept, simply never (direct-cost-of-duplication limit, § 7922.530(a); North County Parents; NLG v. Hayward). The only chargeable labor: time actually spent running the copier, and construction/programming/data-compilation/extraction time for electronic records meeting § 7922.575(b) (billable at full cost including staff time, no statutory cap).

**5.2 Deposits / prepayment.**
Statute is silent on deposits — no percentage caps or deposit scheme. Agencies may condition RELEASE of copies on payment (§ 7922.530(a): records made available "upon payment"), so prepayment of the duplication fee before copying is common and lawful city practice. For § 7922.575(b) programming/compilation jobs, cities commonly require an advance deposit of the estimated full cost — practice inferred from the cost-shifting provision, not an express statutory deposit mechanism.

**5.3 Fee waiver.**
No statutory public-interest or indigency fee waiver anywhere in the CPRA. Waivers/reductions are purely discretionary with the agency (many cities waive small amounts, e.g., under $5-$10, by local policy). Inspection is always free, on-site self-copying with the requester's own equipment is free (§ 7922.530(b)), and emailed existing electronic records are typically free by operation of the direct-cost rule — a de facto no-fee path.

**5.4 PATH: NO PAYMENT.**
Non-payment simply means the agency need not hand over the copies (§ 7922.530(a)); it does not excuse the determination duty. No statutory abandonment rule; cities close requests administratively after a set period of non-payment/non-response (commonly 30-90 days) under local practice. No collections/penalty provisions in the statute.

---

## PHASE 6 — FULFILLMENT & CLOSE

Deliver records by email or download link, with exemption citations for anything redacted or withheld. Closure email; retain the request file (the request and responses are themselves public records).

---

## DEADLINE & CLOCK SUMMARY

**Clock pauses / tolling:** No statutory tolling. The only lawful extension is the single written unusual-circumstances notice of up to 14 days (§ 7922.535(b)); the Act may not be construed to permit delay or obstruction (§ 7922.500), and the First Amendment Coalition notes no further delays are authorized. A cyberattack outage (AB 370, eff. 1/1/2026) supports the extension only until the agency regains access to its servers/systems, and only for records maintained there electronically. In practice, clarification exchanges under § 7922.600 and awaiting deposits for programming jobs pause processing, but that is convention, not statutory tolling.

## REMEDIES

Any person may sue in superior court for injunctive or declaratory relief or a writ of mandate to enforce the right to inspect/receive copies (§ 7923.000); the court sets an expedited schedule (§ 7923.100), may examine records in camera (§ 7923.105), and orders disclosure if withholding is unjustified (§ 7923.110). Trial court orders are reviewable only by extraordinary writ petition to the Court of Appeal, not ordinary appeal (§ 7923.500). Attorney fees and costs are MANDATORY for a prevailing requester (§ 7923.115(a), text verified) — including via the catalyst theory when the suit motivates disclosure (Belth v. Garamendi (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 896; Sukumar v. City of San Diego (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 451); fees are paid by the agency, not personally by officials; the agency can recover fees only if the requester's case is "clearly frivolous" (§ 7923.115(b)). No civil penalties, no damages, no criminal sanctions in the CPRA itself. Agencies cannot preemptively sue requesters for declaratory relief (Filarsky v. Superior Court (2002) 28 Cal.4th 419).

## PORTAL DESIGN NOTES

Portal design for California cities: identity fields must be optional (anonymous/pseudonymous requests are valid; only a delivery email is practically needed); do NOT include a mandatory purpose question (§ 7921.300) except a conditional declaration module for arrestee/victim address requests (§§ 7923.600 et seq.); no commercial-rate module (no commercial branch in CA); no fee-waiver question needed (no statutory waiver — optionally offer a discretionary-waiver note); auto-acknowledgment is good practice but the legally critical automation is a 10-calendar-day determination timer with a one-time up-to-14-day extension letter generator that forces selection of a statutory ground (separate field facilities / voluminous / consultation / data compilation-programming / cyberattack outage / emergency currently-and-directly affecting agency) and requires the written extension notice before day 10; determination letters for granted requests must include an estimated date and time of production (§ 7922.535(a)); denial letters must be written, cite exemptions or § 7922.000 balancing, and auto-insert names and titles/positions of each responsible person (§ 7922.540). Fee engine: $0 default for emailed existing electronic records; per-page direct-cost rate for paper only; block any line items for search/review/redaction time (litigation exposure — NLG v. Hayward); allow full-cost billing plus deposit only on flagged § 7922.575(b) compilation/extraction/programming jobs. Flag as litigation-exposure events: missed day-10 determination (constructive denial + mandatory fee-shifting under § 7923.115), any extension beyond 24 total days, and any fee above direct cost. Weekend/holiday day-10 rollover to the next business day per Code Civ. Proc. §§ 12-12b and Gov. Code § 6700 (settled practice; the CPRA itself is silent).

**Uncertainties / verify locally:** (1) Per-page figures ($0.10-$0.25) are common practice/case-law-approved ranges, not statutory numbers; flat city schedules (e.g., $1.00 first page) are contested by the First Amendment Coalition as exceeding direct cost and have not been definitively adjudicated. (2) The statute says '10 days' without the word 'calendar'; the calendar-day reading (with weekend/holiday rollover via CCP §§ 12-12b) is universal agency and AG practice but not spelled out in the CPRA. (3) Clarification restarting the 10-day clock and closure-after-nonpayment windows (30-90 days) are city practices that vary; no statute governs. (4) Deposit authority for programming jobs is inferred from § 7922.575(b) cost-shifting plus universal practice, not an express deposit statute. (5) Local administrative review (SF Sunshine Ordinance Task Force) exists only in some charter cities — a statewide portal should not assume any admin appeal. (6) 2026-effective legislation verified: AB 370 (Stats. 2025, ch. 34) and AB 343 (Stats. 2025, ch. 142) are the only CPRA changes touching Gov. Code procedure/definitions for cities; other 2026 bills (AB 1147, AB 847, AB 1178, AB 1388, AB 1004, SB 851, SB 635) affect specific record types or entities, not municipal email copy-request deadlines or fees. A mid-2026 session bill could still postdate sources reviewed (checked through July 2026). (7) 'Constructive denial' on a blown day-10 deadline is a litigation/practice concept, not an express statutory deemed-denial rule.
